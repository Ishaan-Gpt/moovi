# "How to Make Viral 3D Stickman Animations With Claude (Free Full Workflow)": the long form, script v2

Essence (PLAN-v2.md): the famous What If channels' own clips on screen as the target, ours made with Claude beside them, and how.
Author's English. One voice (Fish "Ethan"), one continuous read in sections. Every number is traced in the sources at the bottom.

## 1 · proof (their clip beside ours)
This little stickman film got four point six million views in four weeks, on a channel that didn't exist in September, and the one playing next to it, the man with forty-one words left, I made with Claude this morning, and every other tool in it was free. By the end of this video you'll know how both of them were made, where mine fell apart and how I dragged it back up, and you can take the whole system home with you, because I've put it on GitHub.

## 2 · the gold rush
The genre is called What If, and it is barely a month old. On the seventh of October a channel called Ifverse posted a seven-minute film about a kid in a red hoodie whose reaction time is ten times faster than yours, and it has now been watched four point six million times. Within two weeks the others arrived: Minute Zero, with nineteen thousand subscribers, got two point two million views, Ifinite, with twelve thousand, got one point seven million, and a channel called Miko got almost seven hundred thousand views with only four thousand subscribers, which is the kind of number that makes people quit their jobs. Then everybody noticed. Eleven of these videos went up in the genre's first week, fifty-four in the second and a hundred and forty-seven in the third, while the typical new video fell from about two hundred and fifty thousand views to twenty-one thousand, so if you copy what you see, you arrive just in time to share the leftovers with a hundred other people. And yet fifteen of them still passed a hundred and twenty-five thousand views in less than a week, a lot of them from channels you've never heard of, and the gap between those and the copies is what I wanted Claude to find.

## 3 · the study
So I asked Claude to watch the openings of the seven biggest ones the way a film student would, one frame every second, with the narration written out word by word, and to tell me what the hits do that the copies don't. It came back with a rulebook, and the first rule surprised me, because none of them tell you the premise straight away. Ifverse shows you a fly, and then a coffee cup that's caught before it has even started to fall, and only after thirty-five seconds of that does the title appear, because by then you've already seen the power working and you badly want to know how. The second rule is a number on screen, the normal person against you, a quarter of a second against two and a half hundredths, which turns a daydream into something you can measure. The third is a friend who says the line you're thinking, "how did you do that", so you never feel lost, and the fourth is the pace, because Ifverse reads at about one and a half words a second and lets the silences do half the work.

## 4 · the look
The look was the finding that changed my whole plan. I'd assumed the 3D channels were winning, because 3D looks expensive, but the two biggest character films in the genre are flat 2D drawings dressed up to feel 3D, with a rim of sunset light around every black head, a blurred foreground and a soft city behind, while the real 3D character channels sit lower, at around nine hundred thousand. I knew it was true because I'd already tried. Earlier the same day I gave Claude one hour to make a stickman short in proper 3D, it finished one in forty-seven minutes, and two blind judges scored it five out of ten, because the legs couldn't run and the hand couldn't reach the face. So this time we drew it in 2D on a single canvas, the way the top channels do, where nothing has to run or bend, and every shape sits exactly where you want it.

## 5 · the idea
The idea is where most copies die, so Claude searched every premise on my list before we wrote a word, and threw most of them out. "What if you could rewind ten seconds" has been made at least eight times this month, and the best of those copies has thirteen thousand views. "What if you only had a thousand words left for your whole life" hadn't been made by anyone in the genre, and the closest thing on YouTube was a Roblox story with eighty-nine thousand views, which told us people want it and nobody had served it yet. It also keeps the genre's best trick, a number over the head, and it asks nothing of the characters except talking, looking and going quiet, which matters more than it sounds, because every action you can't animate well is a scene you'll end up cutting.

## 6 · the build
The script came next, written the way these channels write, in the second person, with the power proven before it's named: you have forty-one words left, your best friend has just asked you to be his best man, and the whole café is staring at the number over your head. The voices came from Fish Audio's free model, a narrator, a friend and one word from you, and Claude lined up every word to a hundredth of a second, so the counter drops from forty-one to forty on exactly the word "always". The pictures are all code: a café three and a half thousand pixels wide with a sunset in the window, black heads with a lemon rim, a counter that pulses when it ticks, and twenty-one shots that cut every two or three seconds, with music written in code from scratch, so there is nothing to license.

## 7 · the fall
Then I did what I always do, which is hand it to a judge who doesn't know which film is mine. Claude gave a fresh agent the frames of three openings, mine, Ifverse's and Ifinite's, with no names on them, and told it to be strict. Mine came last, with four point three out of ten, and the notes were brutal: the film opened on the back of a black head with no face, the wedding ask was never actually shown, the whole thing sat in a muddy olive light, and the crowd stood around like furniture. The judge was right about every one of them, and this is the moment most people either give up or start polishing the wrong thing.

## 8 · the climb
So we fixed exactly what the judge named and nothing else. Your face and the number open the film now, the ring box sits between the two of you as he asks, the room got brighter, and the crowd got different heights, cups in their hands and one person who gasps. A second judge, who had never seen the first version, scored it five and a half, which put it above the four-point-six-million-view opening in that round, though still behind Ifinite, and still not good enough to ship, because my bar is eight.

## 9 · what I got wrong
I owe you two corrections of my own. My first plan for this video had nothing to do with these channels at all, because I'd built a game show with fifty stickmen and spent a morning on it, until I watched the video that inspired this one again and saw that the whole point was the famous clips on screen and making yours right beside them. I also burned fifteen minutes rendering a video file just so a judge could look at it, when the judge only ever needed the frames, which Claude now pulls straight from the page in about a minute, so the film gets exported once, at the very end, and never before.
